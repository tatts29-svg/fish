How to make the next one better

Three changes, in the order they are worth money. Every figure is what this shut actually did, not a target.

Worth $29,672

Stage the arrivals to first-need date, not to Flame Off

84 line(s) were on site and on charge before anybody used them, at a cost of $29,672. The fix is a date, not a negotiation: ask each crew when they actually need their plant and book delivery to that day. The worst single one on this job sat 15 days before its first job.

Worth $23,036

Challenge the contingency list before it is ordered

61 line(s) never went out to one person and still cost $23,036. Some of that is deliberate cover and should stay. The question for the next order is simply which ones — asked in the planning room, where it is free, rather than at the end where it is an invoice.

Worth $3,561

Off-hire the day it finishes, not the day it leaves

12 line(s) stayed on hire after their last use, costing $3,561. This is the smallest of the three here, which is worth saying plainly: the demob chase is working. The money is at the front of the job, not the back.

No cost

Run this report weekly on the next job, not at the end

Every number here was available from day four. Read weekly, the “came early” bar shows up while there is still time to send something back — at the end of a job it is only a lesson. Nothing needs building: this page rebuilds itself from the same export.

What this does not say. A machine that stood by all week for one lift did its job, and the export cannot tell that from a machine nobody wanted. This counts what SiteIQ charged and what SiteIQ can evidence — the judgement stays with the person who was there. Read it as the question list for the next mobilisation, not a verdict on this one.
